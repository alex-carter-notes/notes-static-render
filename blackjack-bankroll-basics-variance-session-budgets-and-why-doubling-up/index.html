Blackjack Bankroll Basics: Variance, Session Budgets and Why Doubling Up Fails

Alex Carter · 2026-11-25

Two players can follow exactly the same basic strategy at the same table and finish a session in very different places. The reason is variance: results swing far more than the small house edge suggests, and most bankroll mistakes come from treating a normal swing as a sign that something is wrong. This note explains how big the swings are and how to plan around them.

How large the swings are

With sound basic strategy the house edge is roughly 0.5 percent, so the expected loss is about A$5 for every A$1,000 you stake. The swing around that average is much bigger. A single hand has a standard deviation of a little over one bet, because doubles, splits and blackjacks pay unevenly.

Take 100 hands at A$10. You wager about A$1,000, the expected result is a loss near A$5, and one standard deviation is about A$115 (1.15 bets times A$10 times the square root of 100). Roughly two sessions out of three land within that range of the average, which means ending A$100 down or A$100 up is ordinary. Neither result says much about your skill or the site.

Set a session budget first

Decide the amount you are prepared to lose before you sit down, and treat it as the price of entertainment rather than a stake you hope to grow. Keep it separate from rent, bills and savings. Once it is gone, the session ends.

A workable rule is to make one betting unit between 1 and 2 percent of the session budget. With A$200, that is A$2 to A$4 a hand. This gives you 50 to 100 units of cushion, enough to ride out an unlucky run and still have funds for doubles and splits, which temporarily need up to twice or three times the unit on the table.

Why doubling after a loss fails

The Martingale idea, doubling the bet after every loss so that one win recovers everything, is the most common trap. It breaks for two reasons.

  • The numbers grow fast. Start at A$5 and lose seven hands in a row: you have lost A$635 and the next bet is A$640. A run of seven losses has roughly a 1 in 150 chance at any starting point, so over a few hundred hands you can expect to meet one or two.
  • Table limits. Every table has a maximum bet, and the progression usually hits it before it saves you.

Even a perfect system could not change the average, because each hand carries the same negative expectation. Betting systems only reshape how losses are distributed over time.

Habits that help

  • Write down the budget and the time limit before starting, and stick to both.
  • Use deposit limits and reality checks if the site offers them.
  • Stop after a big win as readily as after a loss; neither changes the next hand.
  • Never chase losses with a second deposit.
